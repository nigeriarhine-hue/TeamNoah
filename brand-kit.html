Brand Kit

Noah fixes what’s actually wrong with your Mac, and shows you every step before it does anything.

This is the one document to read before making anything with Noah’s name on it — a campaign, a page, a shirt, an AI-generated image. What Noah is, what we believe, what we say, and what Noah must never look like.

The brand in one minute.

If you read only this section, you should still be able to make something right.

Noah does real work on your computer. You see every change before it happens, and you can undo it.

  • The feeling we sell is relief, not rescue. The user isn’t a victim being saved. They’re a person who was stuck, and now isn’t. Nobody gets carried; they get unblocked.

  • The enemy isn’t CleanMyMac. The enemy is theater. Software that performs work instead of doing it — the scan that finds “1,847 issues,” the progress bar with nothing behind it, the scare that sells an upgrade. We are the opposite of that, and everything we make should feel like it.

  • The proof is that you watch it work. Every action shown before it runs. Your approval is the gate. It’s reversible. The code is open. We don’t ask for trust — we make trust unnecessary by being checkable.

  • Plain English, both directions. You describe the symptom your way; Noah answers in words your parent would understand. No Terminal, no jargon, no translation homework.

  • Why it’s worth doing. Because the alternative is millions of people quietly living with a machine that fights them, believing that’s just how computers are.

The elevator version — say it like this

Your Mac is slow, or full, or something broke after an update. You tell Noah what’s wrong in your own words. It figures out the actual cause — usually not what you’d guess — tells you in one sentence what it wants to do, and doesn’t touch anything until you say go. Then it fixes it, and you can undo it.

What Noah is

You describe what’s wrong. Noah finds what’s actually wrong.

Noah is a macOS app. You describe your Mac’s problem in plain English. Noah runs audited diagnostics to name the actual cause, proposes a one-sentence fix, and runs it only after you tap approve — every change shown first, logged, and reversible.

The near vision

Nobody has to live with a computer problem, because there is always someone who can actually solve it.

The real vision

Humans and their technology work together smoothly.

Our contribution to that: we put real intelligence onto the user’s own computer, and we do it responsibly. Not a chatbot that hands you Terminal commands and wishes you luck — an agent working inside a harness we built, with hard limits, an audit trail, and your approval as the gate.

What we believe

Seven things we don’t trade away.

  1. What Noah is not allowed to do matters as much as what it does.

    Anyone can give an AI root on your Mac. The work — and the value — is in what it is not allowed to do. Show every action before it runs, log it, make it reversible. Never blind-delete.

  2. Anyone can check our work.

    The client is open source. You can watch it work.

  3. Finding the cause matters more than running a scan.

    We are not a cleaner and never will be. Cleaners sell the feeling of maintenance and hand the actual work back to you. Slow is usually a runaway process or a login item — not junk. The cleaner empties the caches and the Mac is still slow. That failure is our entire opening.

  4. We never fake anything to make a sale.

    No fake “at risk!” scares, no invented urgency, no hidden cancel, no progress bar performing work that isn’t happening. Not even if it converts better. Especially not then.

  5. People describe problems in their own words, and that’s enough.

    Nobody should have to translate their symptom into our vocabulary. “My fan won’t stop” is a complete, valid, expert bug report.

  6. We are not IT.

    Never use the word. IT is a helpdesk you file a ticket with, a queue you wait in, a person who sighs at you. Noah serves consumers, in their own words, right now. The feeling we are replacing is not “unsupported” — it is frustration.

  7. The user decides.

    Noah proposes; the human approves. We never take the decision away from the person whose computer it is.

Core messaging

Lead with the symptom. Then the real cause. Then the approval.

In that order, every time. The approval moment — nothing runs until you tap approve — is the single most differentiating thing we have. If a piece of creative has room for exactly one idea, it is that one.

Voice

A competent friend who already looked at the problem.

Calm, plain, specific, unhurried. Not a salesperson, not a wizard.

  • Noah is always third person. “Noah will fix it,” never “I will fix it.” An AI posing as a person doesn’t build trust. First person belongs to the user.

  • Short declaratives. If a sentence needs a comma to survive, it needs a period instead.

  • Name the real thing. “Stale pre-Tahoe preferences” beats “some issues were found.” Specificity is the trust signal — vagueness is what the scam category sounds like.

  • No hype adjectives. Not revolutionary, blazing, powerful, next-gen, or supercharged.

  • No condescension, no self-deprecation. Never imply the user should have known. Never apologize for the product.

The mark

It’s a load line, not a boat.

“The Carrying Tide.” A Plimsoll load-line disc, cut low. Sky and humanity ride above the waterline; the tide — Noah’s intelligence — carries from below. Light is daylight over water. Dark is “Lantern”: a quiet night sky over a tide that glows from within.

Why a load line and not an ark: the Plimsoll line is a safety mark. It exists because ships were being overloaded until they sank, and the fix was a public, regulated, visible limit painted on the hull where anyone could check it. That is exactly our product — a visible limit on power, in a category that had none.

Two colours carry meaning and must not be used decoratively. Commit teal #0D9488 marks a confirmation — something succeeded, something is safe. Amber #D97706 marks a caution. And the Aurora gradient is reserved for the one thing to do next — the primary action, or the phrase the whole page turns on. The moment it becomes wallpaper, it stops meaning anything.

Surfaces

Print and screen can look different. They just can’t contradict each other.

A T-shirt is not a landing page, and forcing one to look like the other makes both worse. What has to stay constant is the identity — the mark, the words, the beliefs, the refusals. What is free to change is the treatment.

Screen — the Aurora system

Site, app, ads, decks

  • Warm cream #ECE8DF ground, warm ink #2A251F
  • Plus Jakarta Sans 700 headlines, tracking −0.035em
  • Instrument Serif italic as the accent voice
  • Aurora gradient on the primary action only
  • Teal confirms, amber cautions
  • Soft cards, 12px radii, low shadows

Print & merch — 2D

Shirts, stickers, signage

  • One or two inks. Garment colour is your ground — don’t fake cream on fabric
  • Use the one-colour mark; gradients screen-print badly and cost per colour
  • Type can be bigger and tighter than the web ever goes
  • No card, no shadow, no radius — those are screen conventions with no meaning on cotton
  • Aurora blue–violet is a screen gradient; on fabric use flat navy or white
  • The sentence is the design — there is no hover, no scroll, no second screen

The test for anything new: would someone who knows Noah recognise this as Noah? If yes, the treatment can be whatever the surface needs. If it only looks like Noah because it copied a gradient, it isn’t there yet.

The essence test

Calm, plain, and honest.

Noah looks calm, spacious, honest, warm. A person who is relieved, not rescued. If an image would look at home on a security-software box or a crypto landing page, it is wrong.

Three things Noah is not. These come up constantly — treat them as settled.

Not this

The ark, the boat, the flood, animals two-by-two

Our maritime reference is the load line, not the ark. An ark means catastrophe-and-rescue — your Mac is dying, be saved — which is the scare playbook of the exact category we refuse to join, just wearing a costume. It also reduces the brand to a biblical pun and invites a religious read we don’t want.

Not this

The superhero, the cape, the mascot who swoops in

That is theater, and theater is the thing we are against. It also casts the user as a helpless victim being rescued — when our entire promise is that they stay in control and press approve. A cape takes agency away from the one person we’ve promised to give it to.

Not this

Cyber, crypto, neon grids, matrix rain, glowing circuit boards, “virtual ID” cards

Wrong three times over. It borrows the visual language of crypto — a trust-poisoned category, fatal for a brand whose only asset is trust. It signals hacker, for technical people, when our buyer is explicitly non-technical and already scared of the Terminal. And it’s cold, when the feeling we’re selling is relief.

Six ways to get it wrong

  • ×Don’t recolour it. Navy with the blue–violet tide, or one flat colour from the files above. Never gold, chrome, or neon.
  • ×Don’t add effects. No drop shadows, bevels, outer glows, or outlines.
  • ×Don’t stretch it. Scale proportionally. The circle is a circle.
  • ×Don’t tilt it. A level line is the entire point of the mark.
  • ×Don’t crop the waterline. It extends past the disc deliberately — that overshoot is the load line. A disc alone is not the logo.
  • ×Don’t crowd it. Leave clear space on all sides at least as thick as the ring’s stroke.
